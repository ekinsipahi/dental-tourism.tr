## The short answer

Start with the dentist, not with the clinic's photographs. Search the dentist's full name. Look at the title after the name and know what it means. Find out where they graduated, and look for the diploma on the clinic wall. Then look at the clinic itself: does it have a name, a Google profile, and is the person who treats you the dentist you were told about? If the answers are vague, and the price is far below everyone else's, the odds are that you have not found a dentist at all.

This is the order we would follow ourselves, and it applies to our clinic as much as to any other. Below is each step, what the Turkish titles mean, what a serious first conversation looks like, and how to check us the same way.

**Key points**

- Search the dentist's full name first. A general dentist (Dt.) may have no publications, and that is normal; a Dr. should have some.
- Dt. is the five-year dental degree; Dr. a doctorate on top of it; Uzm. Dt. a state specialty. "Implantologist" is a master's on implants, not an official title.
- The diploma belongs on the clinic wall. If you have to ask for it, the other signs have usually spoken already.
- A clinic needs a name, a Google profile and dentists you can look up. No name, no profile, an obscure location and a very low price together mean stop.
- A trustworthy clinic asks for your X-ray and your medical history, gives a written preliminary assessment, and makes the final plan in the chair.
- Turkish dental degrees are not automatically valid abroad; what matters for your treatment is that the dentist is qualified and licensed in Turkey.

## Step one: the dentist's full name

Before anything else, get the full name of the dentist who will treat you, and search it. See whether they have published academic work. If nothing comes up, do not panic: a Dt., a general dentist, often has no publications, and that is normal; for a Dr., who has done a doctorate, you would expect to find some. The point of the search is not the papers. It is to establish that a real, identifiable person with a dental degree stands behind the name.

When you arrive, make sure the person treating you really is that dentist. If it is someone else, ask for their full name and look them up too. If there is no time for that, there is no reason to take an unnecessary risk.

## Step two: what the title after the name means

Turkish dentists carry one of three titles, and they are not interchangeable.

- **Dt.** (Diş Hekimi): a dentist with the five-year dental degree.
- **Dr.**: a dentist who has completed a doctorate on top of the degree, roughly five plus four years of study.
- **Uzm. Dt.** (Uzman Diş Hekimi): a dentist who has completed a state specialty programme, also about five plus four years.

"Implantologist" is a word you will see often. It describes a dentist who has done a master's on implants; it is not one of the three titles above, so if a clinic uses it, ask what exactly stands behind it. In our clinic, Dr. Dt. Devrim Biriken Sipahi has practised for more than 35 years; Dt. Ekin Sipahi graduated in 2025. Both names can be searched, which is the whole point.

Find out where the dentist graduated, and look for the diploma on the clinic wall; it belongs there. If there is none, look at the clinic as a whole. Does it have a name? Does it have a Google profile with reviews? Is it clear who the dentists are? Is the person who came to treat you the one you were told about, or someone else? Each unanswered question is a question mark, and they add up.

At the very end you can still ask, "May I see your diploma?" You are entitled to. But if you have reached that point, the other signs have usually told you what you need to know, and the chance that you are sitting in front of someone who is not a dentist is already high. No name, no surname, a clinic somewhere in between places, and a price far below everyone else's: stop and think, because that combination rarely ends well.

## Are Turkish dental degrees valid abroad?

Not automatically. Every country licenses its own dentists under its own law, and unfortunately there is no global system; a Turkish dentist can work abroad only in countries where an equivalence procedure exists, and the same is true in the other direction. For you as a patient the question is a different one: is the dentist treating you in Istanbul qualified and licensed here? That is what the name, the title and the diploma tell you, and that is what the steps above are for.

## What a trustworthy first conversation looks like

You can learn a great deal before you travel, simply from how a clinic answers. In our clinic the messages are answered by the dentists or their assistants, and the first thing we ask for is your X-ray or tomography scan and your medical history: diabetes, medicines that act on bone, blood thinners, corticosteroids, heart conditions, how much you smoke. You receive a preliminary assessment in writing, free of charge, saying what is likely to be possible and how many visits to plan for. What you do not receive is a final treatment plan, because that is made after an examination in the chair; a clinic that sells you a final plan remotely is skipping the step that protects you. [How that assessment works](/blog/planning-your-trip/send-your-dental-x-ray-for-a-preliminary-assessment/) is described in a separate article.

Two more things a serious clinic will tell you without being pushed. First, that implants are rarely finished in one trip: the implant heals for about three months before the final teeth, so [plan for two visits](/blog/planning-your-trip/dental-implants-in-turkey-how-many-visits/). Second, which implant system will be used and that you will receive its identification stickers, so that any dentist in any country can identify your implant later. If a clinic answers both questions plainly, that is a good sign.

## Reviews, references and Instagram

Read the reviews, and read the bad ones first. Our rule of thumb: a clinic with 70 or more reviews and only a few bad ones is generally a safe choice; a clinic with many bad reviews is not, however good its photographs look. Fake reviews are often impossible to tell apart, but a large number of bad ones tells you everything.

Do not expect to be put in touch with previous patients. Patient rights do not allow it, and a clinic that offers it should make you wonder. What a clinic can show you is its work: our patients' stories are on our Instagram, and you can judge the style, natural or not, before you write to us.

## The red flags in one list

- No dentist's name, or a name that cannot be found anywhere.
- No Google profile, or a profile with many bad reviews.
- No diploma on the wall, and discomfort when you ask about qualifications.
- The person who treats you is not the dentist you were promised.
- One thing on the phone, another in the chair: extractions, implants or cutting down all your teeth, at figures several times higher than discussed.
- Talk of extracting teeth and placing implants where saving the tooth was never discussed.
- A final treatment plan sold to you remotely, without an examination.
- A price far below everyone else's, together with any of the above.
